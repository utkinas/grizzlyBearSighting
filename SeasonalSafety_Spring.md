# Spring Safety: Bears Emerging from Hibernation

Bears come out of their dens in **spring (April through mid-June)** hungry, lean, and often with newborn cubs. They focus on finding the first available vegetation and can be unusually bold or defensive.

## What to know this season

- Bears concentrate in valleys and south-facing slopes where snow melts first
- Mother bears with young cubs are **highly protective** — avoid any sow with cubs
- Weak and hungry bears can be unpredictable
- Early vegetation (grasses, forbs, skunk cabbage) draws bears into open areas you might not normally expect them
- Winter-killed wildlife (carrion) attracts bears; keep distance from any carcass

## While hiking

- Make **continuous noise** while hiking, especially in brushy or wind-noisy terrain
- Travel in groups of three or more when possible
- Avoid known bear corridors — ask local rangers before heading out
- Keep dogs on-leash; unleashed dogs can draw a bear investigation back to you
- Avoid traveling cross-country off trails early in the season
- Give any bear a wide berth, especially if cubs are present

## While driving in bear country

- Reduce speed, especially at dawn and dusk
- Watch for roadside grazing in open meadows
- Mother bears often cross roads with cubs — if you see one, more may follow
- Never approach or feed roadside bears

## If you encounter a bear

Open the **Bear Safety** page in this app for species-specific guidance on grizzly, black, and polar bear encounters.

## Why this matters

Spring is a critical, high-encounter period. Bears are rebuilding reserves after months of hibernation, mothers are introducing cubs to their surroundings, and human recreation in the backcountry picks up. Awareness during these weeks protects both people and bears.
